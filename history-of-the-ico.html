The Full History of the ICO: From a Bitcoin Forum Post to Bonding Curves

Before pump.fun, before airdrops, before "utility token" became a punchline — there was a whitepaper posted to a Bitcoin forum, an $18 million ether sale, a $5.6 billion boom, and a federal report that ended the party. Here's the full arc of the initial coin offering, and how its ghost still shapes every launch in 2026.

Published Sept 27, 2026 · 13 min read · Guides Desk

Every token-launch mechanism running today — the bonding curve, the IDO, the exchange-vetted IEO — exists because something before it broke, got regulated, or simply got outcompeted. That something was the ICO: the initial coin offering, the model that turned "sell tokens directly to the internet" into a multi-billion-dollar industry in under four years, then very nearly regulated itself out of existence. It started with a Bitcoin forum post nobody outside a small developer community noticed, and it ended with a federal agency issuing enforcement orders against companies that had barely shipped a product. In between, it funded the blockchain most of crypto still runs on. Understanding today's launch landscape, covered in our Ultimate Guide to Crypto Token Launches in 2026, means understanding what came before it. This is that history.

2013: a Bitcoin forum post starts it all

The first widely credited ICO didn't come from a startup with a pitch deck. It came from a Bitcoin developer named J.R. Willett, who in January 2012 published a whitepaper describing how new protocol layers could be built on top of Bitcoin's blockchain without changing Bitcoin itself — effectively proposing that Bitcoin could host other assets and applications. In July and August 2013, Willett turned the idea into Mastercoin, selling tokens directly to the public in exchange for bitcoin to fund the project's development. There was no hard cap on the raise and no formal legal structure resembling a securities offering — just a whitepaper, a Bitcoin address, and a community willing to bet on it. The sale raised roughly 5,000 BTC, worth about $500,000 at the time. Mastercoin was later rebranded Omni Layer, and it still exists today as infrastructure for asset issuance on Bitcoin, most notably as the original rail behind Tether before Tether expanded to other chains.

Mastercoin's real legacy isn't the project itself — it's the template. Publish an idea, sell tokens against the promise of building it, use the proceeds to fund development before the product exists. Every ICO that followed, for better and for worse, is a variation on that same structure.

2014: Ethereum funds its own blockchain

The idea's biggest early beneficiary was Ethereum itself. Vitalik Buterin, who had briefly worked with the Mastercoin project before deciding a general-purpose smart contract platform needed its own blockchain rather than a layer on Bitcoin, ran Ethereum's public token sale from July 22 to September 2, 2014. The sale offered ether at a sliding-scale price — cheaper in the earliest days, gradually more expensive as the sale progressed — and raised roughly 31,000 BTC, worth about $18.3 million at the exchange rates of the time, according to figures compiled by outlets including Wikipedia's crowdfunding archives and CoinDesk's retrospective reporting on the sale.

Ethereum's crowdsale mattered for a reason that had nothing to do with the dollar figure. Once Ethereum's mainnet launched in 2015, it gave every future project a shortcut Mastercoin never had: a standardized token format (ERC-20, formalized in 2015) that let anyone deploy a fundraising token in an afternoon, without building a blockchain from scratch. Ethereum didn't just run an early ICO — it built the rails that made thousands of later ICOs possible. That's the loop worth sitting with: the most consequential token sale in ICO history didn't fund a token project at all. It funded the infrastructure that made the entire category viable.

2016–2017: the boom

ICOs existed in small numbers for years before they became a phenomenon. What changed in 2016 and accelerated through 2017 was scale: ERC-20 made launching trivially easy, Ethereum's own price was rising, and a wave of projects — some building real infrastructure, many building little more than a whitepaper and a Telegram group — raised money directly from retail buyers with no bank, broker, or regulator standing between the pitch and the payment. Estimates of total 2017 ICO fundraising vary by tracker, but the widely cited figure is upward of $5.6 billion raised across the year, per data referenced by CoinGape and other industry trackers, with some point-in-time tallies from CoinDesk's own ICO tracker putting cumulative 2017 fundraising past $4 billion by mid-December alone.

The boom wasn't evenly distributed. China became one of the most active ICO markets in the world before regulators there moved decisively: by the time Chinese authorities banned domestic ICO fundraising in September 2017, projects in the country had already raised roughly $400 million from about 100,000 investors, according to figures reported at the time. The ban didn't slow the global market much — capital and projects simply moved elsewhere — but it was an early signal of what was coming everywhere else.

The individual sales got bigger as the year went on. Tezos closed a token sale in July 2017 that raised $232 million, a record at the time, per CoinDesk's reporting — before the project spent much of the following year mired in internal leadership disputes and a shareholder class action, a preview of how far ICO hype could outrun actual delivery. Filecoin then broke that record two months later, raising $257 million in September 2017 for a decentralized storage network, according to CoinDesk. The largest of all wasn't a single-day sale but a year-long crowdsale: EOS, run by Block.one, ran from June 2017 to June 2018 and raised roughly $4 billion, the largest ICO on record by a wide margin, per contemporaneous reporting from outlets including CCN and CryptoSlate — a figure later scrutinized by researchers who argued a meaningful share of the apparent demand came from wash trading rather than organic buyers. Together, these three sales illustrate the range the 2017 boom actually covered: legitimate infrastructure bets, chaotic execution, and raises large enough to draw the kind of scrutiny that was about to arrive.

The SEC steps in: the DAO Report and the Munchee order

The regulatory turning point has a specific date. On July 25, 2017, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission issued its report on "The DAO" — the ill-fated 2016 decentralized investment fund built on Ethereum that raised roughly $150 million before an exploit drained a third of it. The SEC's report concluded that DAO tokens met the definition of a security under the decades-old Howey test, and that anyone issuing or trading tokens meeting that definition needed to comply with U.S. securities law regardless of whether the word "cryptocurrency" was involved. Crucially, the SEC didn't charge anyone over the DAO itself — the report functioned as a warning shot, not a prosecution, but it put the entire ICO market on notice that "it's decentralized" was not a legal shield.

The warning became an actual enforcement action five months later. On December 11, 2017, the SEC issued a cease-and-desist order against Munchee, a company planning to raise $15 million by selling tokens tied to a blockchain-based restaurant review app. The SEC found that Munchee's own marketing — which emphasized that token value could appreciate and that a secondary market would develop — was enough to make the tokens securities, independent of whatever utility the app itself might eventually have. Munchee halted its sale and returned the money to investors before a single token was delivered, and the SEC declined to impose a penalty given how quickly and cooperatively the company unwound the offering. The lesson other projects took from Munchee was blunt: how you market a token, not just what it technically does, can be enough to trigger securities law.

2018: peak froth, then the crash

The DAO Report and the Munchee order didn't stop the ICO market in its tracks — if anything, the first half of 2018 was the category's financial peak. Multiple industry trackers reported ICOs raising well over $13 billion combined in just the first five months of 2018, more than the entire prior year by some measures, as later-stage, better-funded projects rushed to raise before scrutiny tightened further. A separate study by Ernst & Young, reported widely at the time, found that more than 10% of the roughly $3.7 billion in ICO proceeds the firm analyzed had been lost to hacks or scams — a figure regulators pointed to as evidence the category needed real investor protections, not just enthusiasm.

Then the broader crypto market rolled over. As token prices fell through 2018, ICO fundraising fell with them, and by 2019 the collapse was severe by any measure: one widely cited research tally put total ICO fundraising in the first quarter of 2019 at roughly $40 million — down from billions per quarter at the 2018 peak, a decline of nearly 100% year over year. The ICO, as a fundraising category, effectively stopped functioning. Buyers who had happily wired ether to an unaudited smart contract in 2017 spent 2019 watching those same tokens trade for a fraction of their sale price, if they traded at all, and new projects found it far harder to raise money on a whitepaper alone. Projects that still wanted to raise money needed a new structure, and the market built one within months.

The shift to IEOs and IDOs

What replaced the ICO wasn't a rejection of the idea — selling tokens to fund a project — but a redesign of who stood in the middle. The Initial Exchange Offering (IEO) put a centralized exchange in that role: Binance Launchpad's early sales, most notably BitTorrent's token offering in early 2019, had the exchange vet the team and tokenomics, handle KYC, and list the token with instant liquidity the moment the sale closed. That due diligence layer — imperfect, but real — was exactly what the raw ICO model never had, and it let exchanges effectively rebuild retail token sales on top of institutions people already trusted with custody.

The Initial DEX Offering (IDO) followed as decentralized exchanges and automated market makers matured, most visibly through 2020 and 2021, with early examples like Raven Protocol's 2019 sale on Binance's DEX helping popularize the term. An IDO removes the exchange gatekeeper again but keeps the instant liquidity: a project deploys a token contract, seeds a pool on a DEX, and trading starts the moment the pool exists, all without any centralized entity reviewing the sale first. Both structures share a common thread with the ICOs they replaced — someone still writes a whitepaper, sets a price, and sells supply — but the reputational and technical scaffolding around the sale changed enough that regulators, exchanges, and buyers treated them as a meaningfully different category. Neither format disappeared, either: IEOs and IDOs both still run today, often side by side with the launchpad model that came after them, depending on how much a given project wants a gatekeeper involved.

Today's launchpads: the ICO's algorithmic descendant

The newest chapter removes the sale entirely. Bonding-curve launchpads — the model pump.fun popularized on Solana — don't have a presale, a whitepaper pitch, or a fixed price at all. A constant-product formula prices the token algorithmically from the very first trade, liquidity builds itself as people buy and sell, and the token "graduates" to a real decentralized exchange pool automatically once it crosses a market-cap threshold, with no human approval required at any step. It is, in a strange way, the logical endpoint of a lineage that started with Willett's Mastercoin whitepaper: each step further removed the human gatekeeper, and the bonding curve removes the last one — there's no team allocation to trust, no sale terms to evaluate, just an algorithm and a chart. The irony is that the ICO never fully disappeared — it just stopped being the dominant model. Security Token Offerings, community sales gated by KYC, and the occasional direct public sale still happen. But the specific thing that made 2017 what it was — anyone, anywhere, wiring crypto directly to an unaudited smart contract in exchange for a whitepaper's promise — is largely gone from the mainstream, replaced first by exchanges, then by algorithms. Whatever launches next will probably follow the same pattern the whole history above suggests: something breaks, gets regulated or outcompeted, and a new mechanism absorbs the demand that never went away.

Fast answers

What was the first ICO?

Mastercoin, launched by J.R. Willett in July and August 2013, raising roughly 5,000 BTC (about $500,000 at the time) with no hard cap. The project was later rebranded Omni Layer.

How much did Ethereum raise in its 2014 crowdsale?

Roughly 31,000 BTC, worth about $18.3 million at the time, over a sale running from July 22 to September 2, 2014.

Why did the SEC's DAO Report matter?

Issued July 25, 2017, it concluded DAO tokens were securities under the Howey test, putting the entire ICO market on notice that federal securities law could apply — without the SEC filing charges over the DAO itself.

What happened in the Munchee case?

The SEC's December 11, 2017 order found Munchee's planned $15 million token sale was an unregistered securities offering because of how the company marketed potential token value gains. Munchee refunded investors before delivering tokens and avoided a penalty.

What replaced the ICO?

Exchange-hosted IEOs, then DEX-based IDOs, and most recently algorithmic bonding-curve launchpads like pump.fun, which remove the sale process entirely.
