Circle Built Arc for BlackRock. Meme Coins Took Over on Day One.

An institutional payments chain with BlackRock, Visa, Mastercard, and DTCC as validators launched this week. Within 24 hours, meme coin launchpads were doing more business than the payments rails it was built for.

Published Sept 17, 2026 · 9 min read · Markets Desk

Circle, the company behind the USDC stablecoin, spent months positioning Arc as serious financial infrastructure — a Layer 2 blockchain built for corporate payments, tokenized collateral, and "real-time money movement," in the company's own framing, with half-second block times and a validator set that reads like a TradFi who's-who: BlackRock, Visa, Mastercard, and the DTCC among them. CEO Jeremy Allaire reportedly called it the most significant launch in Circle's history since USDC itself. Then it went live on September 16, and meme coin traders showed up before the institutions did.

The numbers, both versions of them

Coverage of Arc's opening day tells slightly different versions of the same story, depending on which on-chain data the outlet pulled. CoinDesk reported that Arc processed 7.83 million transactions in its first 24 hours, but only about 624,000 of those were actual USDC transfers — the chain's stated purpose — alongside roughly $82 million in DEX trading volume. Cryptopolitan and BeInCrypto, citing separate on-chain trackers, put total day-one DEX volume considerably higher, at $410.8 million, with meme coin launchpads accounting for 82% of it — about $336.3 million — versus $74.6 million from everything else combined.

Whichever figure you use, the shape of the story doesn't change: on the day a chain built and validated by some of the largest names in traditional finance went live, the activity that actually showed up wasn't institutional settlement. It was speculative token trading, at a scale that dwarfed the payments use case the chain was designed around.

Arguspad and the 97,000-token stampede

The dominant force on day one was arguspad.io, a meme coin launchpad that alone reportedly generated $202.35 million in volume — roughly half of Arc's entire first-day activity — while minting 83,751 of the more than 97,000 tokens deployed on the chain in 24 hours. Minara.fun ($36.4 million) and Tollylabs ($19.65 million) rounded out the next tier. This is a familiar shape if you've been following PumpReport's coverage of DEX markets for meme trading: a handful of launchpad apps capturing the overwhelming majority of activity on a brand-new chain, almost entirely disconnected from whatever the chain's backers intended it for.

The tokens themselves behaved exactly like any other pump.fun-style launch cohort. ARGUS, the largest of the bunch, topped out at a reported $16 million market cap before sliding. TOLLY fell 56% from its highs, LONG dropped 77%, and COOL fell 75% — all within the same news cycle as the launch itself, the same rapid rise-and-fall pattern covered in our Anatomy of a Pump explainer, just running on a chain nobody expected it to run on.

The dog picture

Institutional chains adopting meme culture badly is its own subgenre at this point, and Arc's launch produced a clean example: Circle VP of Product Rachel Mayer reportedly posted an AI-generated image promoting a token called DUKE, describing it as a depiction of CEO Jeremy Allaire's dog. The post drew roughly a million views and a wave of pushback from crypto-native accounts — commentator Abbas Khan's response, "You can clearly see a lot of their team fundamentally misunderstands meme culture," was widely quoted in coverage of the episode. It's a small moment, but it captures something real about this pattern: institutions keep building chains meant to formalize crypto, and the culture that actually shows up treats the formalization itself as the joke.

This already happened once, in July

Arc is not the first "serious" chain to get hijacked by meme coins on launch. Robinhood Chain went live in July 2026 built around tokenized stocks, and — as covered in our Meme Day Trader's Tech Stack piece — meme coin activity became its actual dominant use case instead, largely through a launchpad app called Pons, which at one point generated nearly $6 million in fees in a single 24-hour stretch, briefly outpacing both Pump.fun and Robinhood Chain's own base transaction fees, on a chain that had launched with $568,000 in first-day volume back in July. By that comparison, Arc's opening — whichever of the reported figures you trust — was already an order of magnitude larger from day one.

The pattern is now two-for-two: build a chain with institutional backing and a specific, non-speculative use case in mind, and the traders who actually show up on day one treat it as fresh, low-friction real estate for meme coin launches. It says less about Arc or Robinhood Chain specifically than about how much idle capital and tooling now exists purely to find the newest available liquidity venue and deploy tokens on it within hours of launch — the same search-volume and rank-jump dynamics covered in our trading signals guide, just compressed into a single opening day.

What to actually watch from here

Whether Arc's payments use case ever catches up to its memecoin traffic is a genuinely open question, and one worth revisiting once the initial trading frenzy settles — new-chain launch volume is almost always front-loaded and unrepresentative of steady-state usage. In the meantime, tokens like ARGUS are already showing up on live DEX trending trackers, the same fast-moving, thin-liquidity dynamics our where the next 1000x call comes from piece describes apply directly here: a brand-new chain with a brand-new set of launchpads is, almost by construction, the least-diligenced corner of the market at any given moment.
