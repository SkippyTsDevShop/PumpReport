Crypto Projects Spent a Record $638 Million Buying Back Their Own Tokens This Year

Two platforms account for 90% of it. Exchanges are starting to treat trading fees the way public companies treat retained earnings — funneling them straight back into their own token price.

Published Sept 21, 2026 · 6 min read · Markets Desk
Data as of Sept 21, 2026, covering the 2026 calendar year to date. Buyback totals will continue to change.

Crypto projects spent a record $638 million on token buybacks over the 2026 calendar year — the highest annual total ever recorded for the practice, with peak activity in March and April coinciding with the year's highest trading volumes. Two platforms, Hyperliquid and pump.fun, account for roughly $574 million of that total between them — about 90% — leaving the remaining $64 million spread across every other crypto project combined.

The mechanics: turning fees into a repurchase program

Hyperliquid's approach routes a slice of trading fees from its perpetual futures and spot markets — fees that range from 0.01% to 0.035% depending on the market — directly into automated purchases and burns of its own HYPE token. Pump.fun's version draws from token creation fees plus a percentage of trading activity on its bonding curve, a mechanism its community approved via governance vote in January 2026. Both are structurally the same idea borrowed from public equity markets: instead of distributing free cash flow to holders as a dividend, or simply letting it accumulate, the platform uses it to reduce its own token's circulating supply.

Why this only works for a specific kind of project

A buyback program requires a project to actually generate real, recurring fee revenue — not a token narrative, an actual cash-generating business. That's precisely why Hyperliquid and pump.fun dominate this list: both run high-volume venues (a perpetuals exchange and the dominant meme coin launchpad, respectively) that produce continuous fee income independent of their own token's price. Most crypto projects don't have that kind of revenue engine, which is exactly why 90% of this year's buyback spend concentrated in two platforms instead of spreading evenly across the sector.

What a buyback does and doesn't guarantee

The framing worth being precise about: a fee-funded buyback is a real mechanism, not a marketing gesture — it's an actual, verifiable reduction in circulating supply funded by actual revenue. What it isn't is a guarantee of price support. Removing supply matters more when demand is also present; a buyback during a period of falling interest in a token reduces the sell pressure somewhat but doesn't manufacture buyers. The framing that "fee-generating trading venues now view token repurchases as a central capital-allocation strategy" is accurate as a description of intent — treating fees like retained earnings — but the actual evidence on how much buybacks move price, as opposed to simply slowing a decline, remains mixed.

The more durable signal here isn't the price effect — it's the revenue model. A launchpad or exchange with a buyback program has publicly committed to a specific, checkable relationship between its usage (trading volume, fees generated) and its token's supply, which is a more transparent structure than most token designs offer. Whether that structure survives a real down cycle, when fee revenue itself contracts along with everything else, is the open question worth watching.
